Sensory Lens — Goal and Examples Review

Review method
Vale → Skeptic → Professor → Continuity Auditor → Editor → Vale reconciliation.

Goal
The game is meant to teach selective description. The learner should choose details because they serve mood, character, tension, meaning, or emphasis rather than listing everything that happens to be present.

How the mechanic is supposed to meet the goal
Each round combines a setting, a character state, one loose detail, and a descriptive lens. The learner writes only a few sentences and is explicitly allowed to reject the loose detail if it does not serve the current lens. That rejection is part of the lesson: good description is selection, not inventory.

How the examples support the goal
The game has 10 settings, 10 character states, 10 loose details, and 10 lenses, creating many more than 30 meaningful combinations. The settings range from public spaces to intimate ones; the states include nervousness, homesickness, controlled anger, delight, suspicion, exhaustion, hope, embarrassment, loneliness, and focus.

The lenses force different kinds of selection: sound, touch and temperature, smell, visual contrast, movement, absence, distance, light and shadow, texture, and repetition. The loose details are deliberately specific—a red mitten, a paper crown, wet footprints, a crossed-out note, a damaged pair of glasses—so the writer must decide whether a striking object is actually useful.

A potentially mismatched loose detail is not automatically a defect here because the instructions explicitly allow the learner to ignore it. That makes incompatibility part of the editorial decision being practiced.

Review
The game meets its goal. It is especially strong because it asks the learner to discard material instead of rewarding inclusion of every supplied prompt.

One limitation is that several lenses are broader than literal sensory description: absence, distance, movement, and repetition are really descriptive selection strategies. That is not harmful, but the game is better understood as a selective-description exercise than as a strict five-senses exercise.

Verdict
PASS. The combinatorial bank comfortably exceeds 30 meaningful exercises, and the reject-or-use decision directly supports the learning objective.
