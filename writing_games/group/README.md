# Group Writing Games

## Purpose

Group games are writing-learning games that specifically require collaboration. They belong here rather than in the normal solo-friendly categories because the writing lesson depends on several people holding different pieces of the problem and building one result together.

A game is not a Group game merely because several people can take turns playing it.

**All Group games are cooperative.** Players may control characters whose goals conflict inside the fiction, but the players themselves are never opposing teams and are never trying to defeat, outscore, deceive, sabotage, or eliminate one another. Everyone is working toward one shared writing outcome.

## Group-game test

A strong Group game should satisfy all of these:

1. **Other players are necessary.** One person should not be able to make every important decision alone without losing the point of the exercise.
2. **Players are interdependent.** Each person holds information, responsibility, authority, a constraint, or a resource that the others need.
3. **No one has complete control.** The final scene or story decision must emerge from communication and coordination rather than one player dictating the answer.
4. **Communication is part of the writing lesson.** Talking, negotiating, questioning, revealing, withholding, reconciling, or coordinating should directly exercise the target writing skill.
5. **The group owns one outcome.** The activity should produce one shared scene, solution, story state, or writing plan rather than several unrelated individual answers.
6. **The mechanic teaches.** The collaboration must make the writing concept easier to feel or understand than a solo worksheet would.
7. **Cooperation only.** There are no opposing teams, traitors, saboteurs, hidden enemies, or player-versus-player win conditions. Characters inside the story may oppose one another, but the people playing are all solving the same writing problem together.
8. **Completion, not winners.** The meaningful result is that the group completes the writing problem. There is no individual winner or losing team.
9. **Private information must be justified.** Hidden information should model a real writing problem such as character knowledge, evidence, motive, continuity, or point of view rather than exist only to make the game harder. Private information must help the group build the shared result; it must not turn a player into an adversary.
10. **Authored scenarios are preferred when logic matters.** If clues, timelines, motives, or evidence must fit together exactly, use predetermined reviewed scenarios rather than random combinations that may break coherence.
11. **Longer play is acceptable when collaboration needs it.** The normal five-minute target remains useful, but a true group game may run roughly 10–15 minutes when discussion and coordination are part of the lesson.

## Candidate games

### 1. The Missing Witness
**Writing concept:** information control — who knows what, when they know it, and how information reaches the reader.

Players receive different private pieces of one authored mystery scene. Nobody has enough information to build the scene alone. The group must reveal facts through dialogue, action, questioning, suspicion, and discovery without dumping every fact at once or breaking character knowledge.

All players are cooperating to make the scene coherent. A player may control a suspicious or deceptive character, but that player is not trying to fool or defeat the other players.

Status: first concept to develop.

### 2. Continuity Room
**Writing concept:** continuity and causal coherence.

Each player is responsible for a different continuity domain such as timeline, character knowledge, objects/locations, or promises/obligations. The group receives a scene that develops through several changes and must keep one coherent version alive. A decision that fixes one problem can create a contradiction in another player's domain, so the group must reconcile changes rather than patch them independently.

Collaboration requirement: no player sees every continuity constraint, and everyone is jointly trying to preserve one coherent story.

### 3. Clue Network
**Writing concept:** foreshadowing, evidence, and earned reveals.

Players hold different clues from one authored scenario: physical evidence, testimony, prior behaviour, and contextual facts. Together they must decide what the reader should learn early, what should remain ambiguous, and what later reveal those details can support. The group then builds a short reveal plan that uses earlier details rather than introducing the answer from nowhere.

Collaboration requirement: the reveal only works when the separate evidence types are combined correctly by the group.

### 4. Dialogue Crossfire
**Writing concept:** subtext and character motivation.

Each player controls one character's private want, fear, boundary, and piece of knowledge. The fictional characters may be in conflict, but the players cooperate to build one believable conversation in which nobody simply states the private information. Meaning has to emerge through tactics, evasions, pressure, concessions, and what each character chooses not to say.

Collaboration requirement: each player's character information is needed to make the shared conversation coherent; nobody is trying to "win" the argument as a player.

### 5. Pressure Council
**Writing concept:** escalation through consequences rather than random trouble.

The group controls one shared scene, but different players are responsible for goal viability, relationship pressure, available resources, and public/private information. When the scene changes, the group must choose an escalation that grows from the current story state. A proposed complication is rejected if it does not affect at least one other player's domain or if it could be removed without changing later choices.

Collaboration requirement: escalation must survive checks from several independently held story conditions, and the group jointly decides what belongs in the scene.

## Development priority

Develop **The Missing Witness** first. It has the clearest collaboration mechanic because distributed knowledge is both the reason multiple players are required and the writing concept being taught.

After that, test **Continuity Room** and **Dialogue Crossfire**. They teach different skills and use collaboration in different ways, which will help establish whether the category has enough mechanical variety without introducing player-versus-player structures.
