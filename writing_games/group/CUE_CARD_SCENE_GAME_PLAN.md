# Cooperative Cue-Card Scene Game Plan

## Purpose
Create a fast, cooperative writing game that teaches core story-construction concepts while a small group builds a complete scene together.

The game should feel like a clue-based collaborative exercise rather than a quiz. Players work from static printed cue cards, make choices together, and gradually construct a scene with a clear causal spine.

## Core Constraints
- Cooperative group game.
- Designed for 3–5 players per group.
- A larger room, including up to roughly 12 people, should split into multiple small groups.
- Each small group has its own complete set of phase decks.
- Maximum play time: 15 minutes.
- Every player draws exactly one story card.
- No player draws twice.
- Draws are random.
- A drawn card must be used; there are no redraws.
- All cards are static and printable.
- First version is genre-neutral and must work across genres.
- Regular choice cards present no more than four valid choices.
- The whole group decides which option to use on regular choice cards.
- Each regular story card contains no more than one sentence explaining the writing concept being taught.
- Point of View is mandatory in every game.
- POV cards are not multiple choice: each POV card specifies exactly one POV.
- The mandatory POV draw occurs before player story-card turns and does not consume anyone's one story-card draw.

## Learning Source
Use the current Learning Gamification / Story Construction material as the conceptual source. The game should reinforce the same writing principles, but compress them into a 15-minute cooperative scene-building format.

The important story-construction concepts include:
- Character
- Goal
- Motivation
- Stakes
- Conflict
- Obstacles
- Cause and Effect
- Point of View
- Scenes
- Escalation
- Resolution / Consequence

Not every concept requires its own choice card. Closely related concepts may be combined when necessary to keep the game short and the resulting scene coherent.

Examples of useful combinations:
- Goal + Motivation
- Conflict + Stakes
- Obstacle + Cause and Effect
- Resolution + Consequence

Point of View is the exception: it always has its own mandatory deck and is never combined with another concept.

## Physical Structure
Each phase has its own physical deck of cue cards. Every group receives an identical set of phase decks and plays independently.

Every group also receives a separate **POV deck**. One POV card is revealed before the story-building turns begin.

Cards should be visually distinguishable by phase, for example through card backs, borders, symbols, or labels. POV cards should have their own distinct visual identity so the group can immediately recognize that POV is a scene-wide rule rather than another story choice.

## Mandatory POV Deck
Point of View is established before the scene is built and applies to the entire scene.

### POV Rules
- Every game must use exactly one POV card.
- The POV deck is shuffled before play.
- One POV card is revealed for the group before the first player's story-card turn.
- There is no multiple-choice selection on a POV card.
- The POV shown on the card is mandatory.
- There are no POV redraws.
- The selected POV remains in effect for the entire scene.
- The POV reveal does not count as one of the players' individual story-card draws.
- Each POV card gives a brief one-line description followed by short examples showing what that POV sounds like.

### POV Card Set

To make the differences easy to recognize, the examples below use the same basic moment: Mara places a letter on a table while Daniel asks whether she has read it.

#### First Person
**Description:** The narrator is a character in the scene and uses **I/me**, giving the reader that character's direct experience.

**Example:**
> I put the letter on the table and tried not to look at Daniel.  
> “You read it?” he asked.  
> My stomach tightened. “No.”

#### Second Person
**Description:** The narration addresses the viewpoint character as **you**, placing the reader directly inside that character's experience.

**Example:**
> You put the letter on the table and pull your hand away.  
> “You read it?” Daniel asks.  
> Your stomach tightens. “No.”

#### Third-Person Limited
**Description:** The narration uses **he/she/they** but gives access to one character's thoughts, feelings, perceptions, and knowledge.

**Example:**
> Mara put the letter on the table and forced herself to let go of it.  
> “You read it?” Daniel asked.  
> Her stomach tightened. He already suspected something.  
> “No.”

#### Third-Person Omniscient
**Description:** The narrator can reveal the thoughts, knowledge, or circumstances of multiple characters rather than staying inside only one mind.

**Example:**
> Mara put the letter on the table, hoping Daniel would not notice her hesitation.  
> Daniel noticed immediately. He had already decided she was lying.  
> “You read it?” he asked.  
> “No,” Mara said.

#### Third-Person Objective — Camera-Eye
**Description:** The narration reports only what an outside observer could see or hear and never directly states a character's private thoughts or feelings.

**Example:**
> Mara put the letter on the table. Her hand rested on it for a moment before she pulled away.  
> “You read it?” Daniel asked.  
> Mara looked toward the window.  
> “No.”

### Quick POV Distinction
- **First Person:** I
- **Second Person:** you
- **Third-Person Limited:** one mind
- **Third-Person Omniscient:** multiple minds
- **Third-Person Objective / Camera-Eye:** no minds; observable evidence only

The final printable deck may contain duplicate copies of common POVs if playtesting shows that some POVs should appear more often, but every individual POV card still contains exactly one POV.

## Proposed Story Phases

### 1. Setup
Establish the people and the initial dramatic situation.

Possible concepts:
- Character
- Situation
- Primary goal
- Motivation

The scene should eventually have one clearly identifiable primary goal, even if secondary goals or additional motivations are introduced.

### 2. Pressure / Development
Create genuine opposition and establish why the situation matters.

Possible concepts:
- Conflict
- Stakes
- Obstacles
- Competing needs
- Secondary goals

The conflict must create real pressure. It should not depend on information that the scene has not established.

### 3. Escalation
Increase pressure on the situation that already exists.

Escalation cards are a special design constraint: **every escalation card must work with every valid scene produced by the earlier phases.**

They therefore describe universal story pressures rather than specific events.

Examples:
- Available time becomes shorter.
- Success now carries an additional cost.
- Someone important changes sides.
- New information changes what a character believes.
- A second problem appears and cannot be ignored.
- The consequences of failure become worse.

The group must interpret the escalation within the story it has already created.

### 4. Resolution / Consequence
Every scene must end somewhere meaningful.

The final card forces the group to determine how the scene resolves and what changes because of it.

The resolution should not merely stop the action. It should produce a consequence, changed situation, decision, revelation, loss, gain, or new condition.

## Player Turn Structure
1. Players establish a turn order.
2. The POV deck is shuffled and one mandatory POV card is revealed for the whole group.
3. The group reads the POV description and example aloud; that POV becomes a rule for the entire scene and cannot be redrawn.
4. The appropriate story-phase deck is shuffled.
5. A player draws one story card and reads it aloud.
6. The card briefly explains the writing concept in one sentence.
7. A regular choice card presents up to four valid options.
8. The entire group discusses the choices and agrees on one.
9. That choice becomes part of the scene and must remain consistent with everything already established, including the mandatory POV.
10. Play moves to the next player.
11. Nobody receives another story-card draw after taking their turn.
12. The final player draw must produce a Resolution / Consequence.

## Scaling for 3–5 Players
The exact number of cards in each story phase is not fixed. Story consistency determines how concepts are combined.

The mandatory POV card is always added before these story-card draws and does not alter the number of player turns.

### 3 players
0. Mandatory POV card for the group.
1. Setup card containing enough information to establish the scene.
2. Conflict / Pressure / Escalation card.
3. Resolution / Consequence card.

### 4 players
0. Mandatory POV card for the group.
1. Setup.
2. Pressure / Conflict.
3. Escalation.
4. Resolution / Consequence.

### 5 players
0. Mandatory POV card for the group.
1. Setup.
2. Development or additional Goal / Motivation element.
3. Pressure / Conflict.
4. Escalation.
5. Resolution / Consequence.

The final card architecture should be tested to ensure every player count produces a complete, understandable scene rather than simply following a rigid phase quota.

## Card Design Rules
Every card should satisfy all applicable rules below.

1. **Universally usable within its phase.** A legal draw cannot make the scene impossible to continue.
2. **Static but adaptable.** Cards cannot depend on dynamically generated text or a particular earlier card.
3. **Specific enough to matter.** Choices should create meaningful story consequences rather than vague prompts.
4. **Generic enough to combine.** Avoid genre-locked details or choices that require a particular setting, technology, relationship, or world.
5. **No false choices.** All options on a regular choice card are valid writing choices.
6. **Maximum four options on choice cards.** The group should be able to read, discuss, and decide quickly.
7. **One-sentence teaching note.** Teaching is embedded into play without slowing the session.
8. **Preserve continuity.** New choices add to or reinterpret established material; they should not casually overwrite it.
9. **Cause-and-effect matters.** Later developments should arise from, complicate, or meaningfully affect what has already happened.
10. **Escalation must genuinely escalate.** Escalation should increase pressure, cost, urgency, uncertainty, opposition, or consequence rather than simply adding unrelated activity.
11. **POV is mandatory and singular.** Every POV card contains exactly one POV. It is drawn before story construction and applies to the entire scene.
12. **POV cards teach by example.** Each POV card contains one brief description and an example demonstrating the viewpoint in use.

## Desired Story Spine
By the end of play, the scene should make this structure recognizable:

**Someone wants something → for a reason → something opposes them → the situation gets worse → a consequential choice or outcome occurs → something changes.**

The POV determines the lens through which the group presents that story spine.

## Facilitation Philosophy
The facilitator should not need to explain every concept manually. The cards should contain enough teaching information for groups to work independently.

The facilitator's role is mainly to:
- distribute decks;
- keep the game within the 15-minute limit;
- enforce the one-draw-per-player rule;
- ensure the mandatory POV remains consistent throughout the scene;
- ensure groups actually integrate each drawn card rather than ignoring inconvenient material;
- lead any optional discussion afterward.

## First-Version Scope
Build only the genre-neutral core game first.

Do not add genre-specific decks yet. Horror, romance, fantasy, mystery, science-fiction, or other expansion decks can be considered after the core mechanics have been tested and shown to produce coherent scenes consistently.

## Example 4-Player Round

### Mandatory POV Draw — Third-Person Limited
**Description:** The narration uses “he,” “she,” or “they” while staying inside one character's perceptions, knowledge, and internal experience.

**Example:**
> Mara put the letter on the table and forced herself to let go of it.  
> “You read it?” Daniel asked.  
> Her stomach tightened. He already suspected something.  
> “No.”

The group must now construct and describe the entire scene through one character's limited viewpoint.

### Phase 1 — Setup
**Teaching note:** A scene works best when a character wants something specific.

**Drawn card: Goal + Motivation**

Choose one:
1. Get someone to tell the truth because the character needs to protect someone.
2. Recover something important because losing it would expose a mistake.
3. Convince someone to stay because the character fears what will happen if they leave.
4. Reach a place in time because someone else is depending on them.

**Group chooses:** Recover something important because losing it would expose a mistake.

The group decides the viewpoint character is trying to recover a missing envelope before anyone discovers she misplaced it.

### Phase 2 — Pressure
**Teaching note:** Conflict appears when something meaningfully prevents the character from getting what they want.

**Drawn card: Conflict + Stakes**

Choose one:
1. Another person wants the opposite outcome, and failure will damage an important relationship.
2. Someone refuses to cooperate, and failure will reveal something the character wanted hidden.
3. The character must choose between two incompatible priorities, and either choice has a cost.
4. The obvious solution is no longer available, and failure will hurt someone else.

**Group chooses:** Someone refuses to cooperate, and failure will reveal something the character wanted hidden.

The group decides a coworker has the envelope but refuses to return it until the viewpoint character explains why it matters.

### Phase 3 — Escalation
**Teaching note:** Escalation increases pressure without replacing the original conflict.

**Drawn card: Escalation**

Choose one:
1. The available time suddenly becomes shorter.
2. Someone new becomes involved.
3. Success now carries an additional cost.
4. New information changes what the character believes.

**Group chooses:** Someone new becomes involved.

The group decides the manager walks into the room and asks what everyone is looking for.

Because the scene is Third-Person Limited, the group may describe what the viewpoint character thinks about the manager's arrival, but not directly state what the manager or coworker privately thinks.

### Phase 4 — Resolution
**Teaching note:** A scene should end with a choice, consequence, or change.

**Drawn card: Resolution + Consequence**

Choose one:
1. The character succeeds, but pays a cost.
2. The character fails, but learns something important.
3. The character changes tactics and creates a new problem.
4. The character gets what they wanted, but not for the reason they expected.

**Group chooses:** The character succeeds, but pays a cost.

The viewpoint character admits in front of the manager that she misplaced the envelope. The coworker returns it, but the mistake is now public.

### Final Scene Spine
- **POV:** Third-Person Limited.
- **Character wants:** the envelope back.
- **Why:** she wants to hide her mistake.
- **Conflict:** the coworker refuses to return it without an explanation.
- **Stakes:** the mistake may be exposed.
- **Escalation:** the manager enters.
- **Resolution:** the character admits the truth and gets the envelope back.
- **Consequence:** she solves the immediate problem but loses face with the manager.

This demonstrates the target result: one mandatory POV card, four random player story cards, four group decisions, and one coherent scene with cause and effect presented through a consistent narrative viewpoint.

## Next Build Step
Design the complete phase/deck architecture and create the first printable cue-card set.

Before considering the deck complete, test it against multiple random combinations for 3-, 4-, and 5-player groups to verify:
- every draw can be used;
- the story remains internally consistent;
- the mandatory POV remains usable and consistent;
- escalation works regardless of setup;
- groups reach a meaningful resolution;
- the game remains playable within 15 minutes.
