# The Missing Witness

## Core writing concept

**Information control:** who knows what, when they know it, what they believe, and how information is revealed to the reader through scene action rather than exposition dumps.

The mystery is the vehicle. The lesson is managing distributed information while keeping character knowledge consistent.

## Players

Designed for **4 players** by default.

It may flex to **3–5 players** by merging or splitting information roles, but every version must preserve the central rule: no player may hold enough information to solve or dictate the scene alone.

## Target duration

About **10–15 minutes**:

- roughly 2 minutes to read the shared setup and private role information;
- roughly 7–10 minutes to build/play the scene;
- optionally 2–3 minutes to compare what each person knew and identify how the information reached the final scene.

The comparison is useful but should not become a mandatory classroom-style debrief.

## Scenario model

Use **predetermined authored scenarios**.

Each scenario contains:

- one shared situation visible to everyone;
- one clear scene goal;
- a coherent underlying truth;
- separate private role packets;
- a known information timeline establishing who could reasonably know each fact;
- evidence or observations with believable sources;
- at least one mistaken belief or incomplete inference that can be corrected through play;
- a final state that can be reached without any player needing to invent missing operational facts.

Random selection may choose which authored scenario the group receives, but clues, roles, evidence, and truth should not be independently shuffled unless the resulting combination has been explicitly authored and reviewed as coherent.

## Default four-player role structure

The exact roles may vary by scenario. A useful default pattern is:

1. **Witness knowledge** — what was actually observed, including limits or uncertainty in that observation.
2. **Investigator belief** — what a central viewpoint character currently believes and why that belief is reasonable but incomplete or wrong.
3. **Hidden motive / private fact** — something a character is concealing that matters to interpretation but is not automatically proof of the central mystery.
4. **Evidence / environment** — physical, documentary, procedural, or contextual information available in the scene and what it legitimately implies.

No role should simply possess "the answer." The truth should emerge from the relationship among several pieces.

## Play loop

1. The group reads the shared setup.
2. Each player privately receives their role information.
3. Players begin constructing one scene together.
4. Information may enter the scene only through a believable action: dialogue, observation, questioning, contradiction, discovery, inference, or a character choice.
5. Players may not simply announce their private role card as out-of-scene information.
6. When one fact changes the interpretation of another, the group must preserve what characters previously knew and believed rather than retroactively rewriting knowledge.
7. The scene ends when the central situation has meaningfully changed and the important information has been integrated coherently enough for the group to understand what happened and why.

## Completion test

The game is complete when:

- the scene can be followed without access to the private role cards;
- important facts reached characters through believable sources;
- no character uses information they could not know;
- at least one early fact or belief changes meaning after later information appears;
- the final understanding depends on combining information held by more than one player;
- the group did not have to stop the scene and simply explain the solution out of character.

There is no score and no winner.

## Example shape

Shared setup: A person disappeared after a community event. A detective questions three people who were in different parts of the building during the relevant ten minutes.

Possible distributed information:

- one player knows a witness saw someone carrying a distinctive umbrella but could not see the person's face;
- one knows the detective assumes a locked side door proves nobody left through it;
- one knows a suspect is hiding an unrelated argument that gives them a reason to lie about where they were;
- one knows the side door automatically locks when it closes and that a rain-soaked umbrella was later found in a storage room.

The authored truth must make every clue, lie, assumption, and physical fact compatible. The group has to bring those pieces into the scene naturally and revise interpretations as information changes.

## Scenario-writing requirements

Every permanent scenario should pass the normal Swamp review pipeline and especially the Continuity Auditor.

Check:

- What exactly happened?
- Who directly observed each fact?
- Who only heard, inferred, assumed, or was told something?
- When could each person reasonably have learned it?
- Why does the issue become relevant now?
- Does every clue have a source and a limited meaning?
- Could one player's packet solve the entire scenario alone? If yes, redistribute information.
- Is a hidden fact merely withheld from players, or is there a believable reason the characters themselves do not all know it?
- Can the group reveal the truth through scene action rather than exposition?

## Content-bank target

Before becoming a canonical active game, build at least **30 meaningfully distinct authored scenarios**. They should vary not only in setting and names but also in information structure: mistaken identity, unreliable observation, timing, conflicting testimony, hidden motive, procedural evidence, physical evidence, innocent concealment, ambiguous wording, and other genuinely different information-control problems.
